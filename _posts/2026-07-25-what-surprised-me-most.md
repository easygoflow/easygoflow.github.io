Learning three languages in parallel wasn't a plan so much as something that happened because I loved three very different things at the same time. A year in, a few things have surprised me enough that I want to write them down.

## The cross-contamination is real

More than once, reaching for a word mid-sentence, a French one has surfaced when I was trying to speak Hindi, or a Japanese sentence structure has bled into how I try to phrase something in French. It's disorienting in the moment, but it's also a strangely good sign — it means the languages are actually living somewhere active in my head, not just sitting in separate, sealed compartments.

## The rhythms are genuinely different

Hindi's rhythm, to my ear, moves in waves — long emphasis, then a quick release. French flows in a more even, connected stream, thanks to liaison stitching words together. Japanese's pitch-accent system is subtler than either, and the easiest of the three to get quietly wrong without realizing it, since English doesn't really have an equivalent to listen for.

## What I didn't expect: teaching helps and hurts in equal measure

As an ESL teacher, I catch myself over-explaining grammar to myself in ways a native speaker never would — analyzing a sentence structure instead of just absorbing it the way I'd want a student to. It's a habit I'm still trying to unlearn in my own learning, even while I teach the opposite instinct to my students every day.

## The throughline

Different rhythms, different scripts, different cultural entry points — and somehow the same underlying method works for all three. That's the part I didn't expect going in, and it's the part that's kept me doing this for a year now.
